A junior of mine from college sent me this video. It's from NPTEL, an IISc professor honestly asking his students why they come to class to learn from a professor when they can "just open up Claude and ask it questions."

I'll be honest, the first time I watched the video it left me really annoyed. It could be because I have the gene of the skeptic. It could also be because I was having a peaceful lunch in the office cafeteria. But I was irritated with the existentiality of it nonetheless. I told my junior I'll respond to him later.

A second, complete watch of the video left me in appreciation of the professor raising his questions and sharing his voice. There are a lot of questions that aren't related to pedagogy but still stem from it, and naturally so! AI hasn't _only_ affected the field of education, and something with such a large influence is bound to have tangential thoughts. I've noted down these instances too.

The professor shares how he's been having sleepless nights thinking about the future. "What should I teach my children? What's the need for education? If intelligence is commoditized, what is the purpose of humanity?" It's a lot of big questions dropped on the shoulders of young adults who are yet to graduate.

I've marked out paraphrased quotes that stood out to me, that I feel like responding to. To quote what I wrote after my first watch:

> I don't think I have the answers and I don't claim to know more than the professor, but I'm still annoyed by how end-of-the-world-y the video is. What if I'm wrong about all this? Who cares! Humanity isn't a Python script running to reach an objective solution.

### What would the economy be based around?

The professor starts with a broader question than the impact on education.

<h4 class="w-4/5 mx-auto italic text-center pb-4">"What do you think will happen if intelligence is commoditized?"</h4>

He outlines how the modern-day economy is built around intelligence. There has been a semblance of a level playing field due to this idea of meritocracy; acquiring knowledge would need effort and time, a currency built through consistency and hard work.

<h4 class="w-4/5 mx-auto italic text-center pb-4">"We had a way to create hierarchy in society based on skill.<br>Take that away from humans, now what?"</h4>

I would actually like to learn the answer to this myself. If intelligence _does_ become ubiquitous, what will we find as our new center of hierarchy? I do think there will still be one; we have always used that which is different to compare and classify people, both positively and negatively. I'll discuss the political side later.

My counter-point here though is that it _isn't_ ubiquitous yet. There's still value to being an expert, a veteran of your field. Take your own example from the past, professor.

<h4 class="w-4/5 mx-auto italic text-justify pb-4">"When I started teaching first at IIT Delhi, I used to watch a lot of videos (from MIT OpenCourseWare, Stanford, etc.) and read a lot of books. Every 1-hour class would take 15-16 hours of preperation..."</h4>

You built your knowledge based on the work of giants, people who dedicated their entire life to very pinpoint questions (and a lot of the times, didn't get the complete answer). Humans have worked like that for a long time.

I will not claim to know more than you about Machine Learning [for] [obvious] [reasons]. But from the little I do know, LLM's as statistical models depend a lot on the data they train from. Data that was written meticulously by experts,

#### Everything Else

#### _"I've never done this before, by the way. In 10 years of teaching I've never diverted a class to a discussion like this."_

I'm really glad that you did, sir. I got to sort out some thoughts that had been churning around in my own head. I do think this lecture could have been less of a burden on the youth than it is, though. I can tell my juniors in college are already going through a rough time with placements.

I have nothing against this guy, but somewhere it feels like this guy has had a disconnect with the real inquisitive curiosity of children. Maybe it's because college students don't display the enthusiasm as vividly, or because a lot of JEE students today are doing it out of a desperation of getting the brand value of a national institute. But this man seems to have lost touch with _why_ we learn.
