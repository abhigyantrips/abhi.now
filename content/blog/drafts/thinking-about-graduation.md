By all means, I have already graduated. I have given my final presentation and done the exit form shenanigans, and received some form of paper from the college that says, "You have done the academic work. We will let you know the rest later."

I have already been a working individual for over 6 months, thanks to how engineering colleges work. It's good for my growth after college, which [I've already begun to experience](/weeknotes/2026/35) after converting. But I also think it blurred the line between college ending and work starting for me, which led me to being surprised when my final project presentation completed and it was just... over.

[a picture from college life]

I was recently chatting with someone and started recalling the beginning of my hostel stay. Everything was new and alien and away from home.

Every year of college felt like it was years long. I would pack up all my stuff after each final, say goodbye to all my friends and take a bittersweet flight home. The subsequent two-month break would feel like its own season, and I'm sure my parents saw me change with every visit I made back dragging a suitcase behind me.

[some other picture]

The first year was spent settling into place. My roommates seemed very different from me; they would sleep much later, had different interests, and maybe even had different standards of "tidiness" compared to me.

We learned much from one another. We helped each other out with studies from time-to-time, hid each other's kettles when the warden came to check the room, and learned our boundaries. We left for home at the end of the year with hugs and group photos.

This was also the year where I met my core group of friends for the rest of college. They [gave me a name](/blog/the-origin-of-pog) that would follow me even after graduation. I finally met Shashaank (a Discord friend) and our nerdy conversations at dinner brought us together with Raghav, whom I collaborated with on basically all my college projects.

We looked very different back then.

[the photo]

Second year was the year of chances. I'd spent the first year hating academics because of physics/chemistry/biology; this year started the Computer Science syllabus, so it was finally my chance to shine. I also applied to be the President of the coding club (CodeX) and -- after a meticulous pitch deck to the faculty -- received an email congratulating me for getting it.
