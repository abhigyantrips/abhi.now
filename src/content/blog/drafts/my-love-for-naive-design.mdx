Do you remember [skeumorphism](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/skeuomorphism/)?

The era where everything aimed to look realistic. From app icons to desktop windows, complex shapes and 3D rendering techniques doing their best to replicate real-life elements on a digital surface.

[image of ios skeumorphic icons]

One part of it that I still like to hold on to was MP3 players. All these different and cool UI's you could pull up on your computer just to play music. Not one of them was "basic", every single one struck out with its own unique personality. That spirit is something I still try to carry with this Android app called [Classipod].

[image of audio players, talk about danq]

I think we all eventually came to a consensus where we put the skeumorphism to rest. We understood that this attempt to bring the 3D to the 2D compromised on accessibility, and was constrained by the physical limits that real-life objects have.

So we ditched it. We introduced flat, "material" UI. Lesser digital shadows and more shape uniformity. Buttons that changed color instead of moving "in" and "out" when pressed. We were no longer mimicking limited physical interfaces, so we got used to UI's having infinite scrolls and extremely vibrant colors; software slowly losing its connection from its roots, which was reality.

It all logically makes sense; we were coming from a time where computers needed to look less alien and more at-home, and the best way to do that was to make software look like things you would have in your house.

We're much more familiar with technology now, where smartphones exist in everyone's hands and apps are monetarily free to install. But looking at the same minimalist icons and button layouts on slightly different shades of white/black everywhere I go, I start wondering what it was we really gained from it.

---

When I first started working on this site in January, I already knew I didn't want it look like everything I was sick of. I wanted it to be as if you were opening _my_ notebook, _my_ photo album, coming to a corner of the Internet that wished you "Good Morning!" with a heartwarming _chai_ ready for you.

I think I manifested it hard enough, because it didn't take much rabbithole-ing for me to find all the elements that brought the current design of this site together. I've gotten all the un-uniformity and doodle-iness I wanted for this place, and it has made me feel cozy every time I have visited it myself.

Once the design was set up, I kinda just left it there and didn't think about it. It was only recently that I learned about this term called "Naïve Design", referring to the trend of "anti-perfect" design elements that have come up in the recent year as "The Rise Against The Machines".

Now that I know that I'm an unintentional ambassador of this trend, I have taken it upon myself to collect and showcase my fragment of it. With this, I welcome you to everything I love about naïve design.

---

### Handwritten Fonts

I see the creation of handwritten fonts as a top-notch exercise of free will. Ever since Comic Sans showed that you don't need to have perfect curves, the font world has seemed to have stopped bodyshaming the alphabet.

My choice for the site was "Sour Gummy" by Stefie Justprince, after going to [Fontsource](https://fontsource.org/) and looking for a font that was close to my own handwriting. There are actually [many options to choose from](https://fontsource.org/?classifications=handwriting) now, with many of them being less quirky and more readable. I love it when designers execute their ideas and open-source them for the love of it.
